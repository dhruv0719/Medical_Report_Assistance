# Hematocrit
A hematocrit test is a blood test that measures the percentage of your blood that is made up of red blood cells. Red blood cells are crucial as they carry oxygen from your lungs to the rest of your body. Hematocrit levels that are too high or too low can be a sign of a blood disorder, dehydration, or other medical conditions. This test is also known as HCT, packed cell volume (PCV), or Crit.

A hematocrit test is often part of a complete blood count (CBC), which is a common test used to check your general health. It can help diagnose or monitor blood disorders like anemia (too few red blood cells) or polycythemia (too many red blood cells). Your provider may order this test during a routine checkup or if you have symptoms of a red blood cell disorder.

Symptoms of anemia (too few red blood cells) may include shortness of breath, weakness or fatigue, headache, dizziness, or an irregular heartbeat (arrhythmia).

Symptoms of polycythemia (too many red blood cells) may include headache, feeling light-headed, weakness, skin itching, heavy sweating, or blurred vision.

Your hematocrit test result is a percentage. For example, a result of 42 means that 42% of your blood consists of red blood cells. Normal levels can vary depending on sex, age, and even the altitude where you live.

A lower than normal hematocrit level can be a sign of anemia, which means your body doesn't have enough healthy red blood cells. It may also indicate recent or long-term blood loss, or that your body is not absorbing enough vitamins or minerals. In some cases, it can be related to bone marrow diseases or certain cancers.

A higher than normal hematocrit level often indicates dehydration. It can also be a sign of a disorder that causes your body to produce too many red blood cells, such as polycythemia vera. Certain lung or heart diseases, or living at a high altitude where oxygen is lower, can also cause high hematocrit levels as your body makes more red blood cells to compensate.

If your hematocrit results are not in the normal range, it does not always mean you have a medical condition needing treatment. For example, pregnancy can naturally cause a lower hematocrit because the body holds more fluid. Always discuss your results with your healthcare provider to understand what they mean for your specific situation.
